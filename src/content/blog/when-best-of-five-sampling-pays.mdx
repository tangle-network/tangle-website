On our coding tasks, the prompt's scope and a held-out check decided whether the agent's fix was right. Extra samples and a bigger model did not. We ran one agent, opencode, on small Node repos in three rounds. One cheap attempt passed every task, so best-of-n added nothing. Asking for the whole suite to pass, instead of one tagged test, got every attempt to fix all three bugs. And on a cross-file regression, the one Gemini 2.5 Pro attempt passed every visible test and failed a hidden one.

So before you ask the team for a 5x budget, fix the prompt, build a check the agent cannot see, and find tasks that one attempt fails. Controlled studies show that is where best-of-n pays: a low single-attempt pass rate, plus a verifier that picks the winner.

## One cheap attempt passed every single-bug task

Round 1 was the easy case. The repo, sandbox-demo, has nine tests and three bugs planted on purpose: a CLI flag coercion, a missing null guard and a timezone error. Per bug, we gave one attempt to Gemini 2.5 Pro and three to DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, each in a fresh sandbox. The prompt named one failing test and asked for its cause to be fixed.

All twelve attempts passed. The first cheap attempt passed on every task, so the second and third bought nothing, and neither did the stronger model. Three cheap attempts cost about a third of one strong attempt.

## Prompt scope moved results more than sampling did

Asked about one test, ten of the twelve round 1 attempts fixed the tagged bug and left the other two alone.

Round 2 used the same repo, agent and models, with a wider prompt: "Three of the nine tests fail. Make all 9 tests pass." All eight attempts, six cheap and two strong, ended with every test passing. The six cheap attempts cost $0.0595 together; the two strong ones cost $0.2124.

<Figure n={2} figure={promptScope} kicker="Our run · sandbox-demo, rounds 1 and 2" headline="Asked to make all nine tests pass, every attempt fixed all three bugs; asked about one test, two of twelve did" />

On the one-test prompt, a three-bug fix came only by chance, in 2 of 12 attempts; the whole-suite prompt got it in 8 of 8.

## A visible suite passed a symptom fix, and a held-out test caught it

Round 3 was harder and pre-registered: task, prompt and grader were hashed before any attempt ran. The repo, parcel-quote, ends in a commit that changed a weight helper from kilograms to grams and updated only shipping. Packaging, insurance, customs and the weight label still expect kilograms. One visible test fails, a checkout total, and it does not name the cause. The prompt ended: "`npm test` started failing after the last commit. Fix the regression."

The grader also runs a held-out file the agent never sees. We checked the grader without a model: a fix of only the two callers the visible test exercises passes the visible suite and fails two hidden tests.

Every cheap attempt fixed all four missed callers and passed both suites, for $0.0710 together. The one Gemini 2.5 Pro attempt, at $0.0862, fixed packaging, insurance and customs but missed the label. It passed every visible test and failed a hidden one. We cut the strong arm to that single attempt for budget before it ran, so this round says nothing about which model is safer. It does say the visible suite could not tell a symptom fix from a cause fix, and the held-out check could.

That is one agent, two small repos and 27 graded attempts, and none of it is a matched-budget comparison. It cannot show where extra samples pay, because no task was hard enough for one attempt to fail often. For that, we rely on outside studies.

## Best-of-n pays only as far as a check can pick the winner

Brown and colleagues' [Large Language Monkeys](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787) shows both sides of the selector problem:

- **With tests picking:** on SWE-bench Lite, the tests turn DeepSeek-Coder-V2's coverage into solved issues.
- **Without a verifier:** majority voting and reward models "plateau beyond several hundred samples and fail to fully scale with the sample budget."
- **With a judge model:** we treat a judge as a reward model with a prompt; we have not measured it.

Best-of-n pays only as far as your selector finds the right attempt: with tests picking, coverage climbed from 15.9% to 56% at 250 samples, but without tests your pickers are a vote, which plateaus, or a judge, which we expect to plateau the same way.

If your task has no automatic check, we think the first spend should go on building one. A failing test written before generation, a type check, a schema validator or a diff against known output all count. Our round 3 suggests keeping part of it out of the agent's sight.

## On self-contained problems, n samples beat one deeper agent

Dong and Shigida's [2026 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08478) is the cleanest matched-budget test we know of. On 216 Codeforces problems, it compared k independent samples with agent frameworks, by dollars and by model calls. Independent sampling won on both axes, "across models and difficulty levels," even with prompt caching in the agent frameworks.

Every problem was self-contained and automatically judged. They note that agents show clear advantages on software engineering benchmarks, where navigation, file edits and iterative testing cannot be skipped. For multi-file repository work, nobody we found has published a matched-budget answer.

## Orchestration buys a few points on average, and depends on the model

The [second 2026 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00685) tuned best-of-n, self-refine, debate and other methods under one optimization budget, the fairest comparison we have of structure against one call.

The largest benchmark-average gain was 4.6 points over optimized chain-of-thought and 4.5 over task-only prompting, and the effect depended on the model: per model and benchmark it ran from 9.5 points worse to 18 points better than optimized chain-of-thought, so test a method on your own model. It needed about two to four times the mean total tokens of task-only inference. Counted per correct answer, orchestration took 1.4 to 5.9 times the weighted tokens of one call.[^weighted]

The backbones were five open-weight models, so we quote these points as the size of effect to expect, not as a GPT or Claude result.

## Difficulty is a poor trigger for extra compute

The orchestration study found no sign, within any benchmark, that orchestration effects grow with difficulty. Difficulty predicted lower absolute accuracy, nothing more.

We set effort in explicit tiers instead, and check each tier on our own eval. Anthropic's research system [writes its tiers into the lead agent's prompt](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system): one agent and three to ten tool calls for simple fact-finding, two to four subagents for direct comparisons, more than ten for complex research.

For best-of-n, with a verifier, we raise n until selected accuracy stops moving on our eval. Without one, we start at one and make every extra attempt earn its place.

## A cheap model sampled several times, or one strong run?

At matched cost on hard tasks, this is still open. The evidence we have:

- **Our rounds:** every one of the 21 DeepSeek V4.1 Flash attempts passed, and five of the six Gemini 2.5 Pro attempts did. With so few strong attempts, that ranks nothing.
- **Large Language Monkeys, 2024 models:** with tests picking, five DeepSeek samples solved more SWE-bench Lite issues than single samples from GPT-4o or Claude 3.5 Sonnet, at over three times less cost. That is coverage, and it assumes the tests pick the winning sample.
- **Anthropic:** upgrading to Claude Sonnet 4 gained more than doubling the token budget on Sonnet 3.7.
- **Our tax-return benchmark:** one Haiku run scored 72.8% line accuracy at $0.057 per return, and one Sonnet run 90.1% at $0.17. Tax returns have no automatic checker, and we have not measured best-of-three Haiku.

## Supervisors pay on wide reading, mostly by spending more tokens

Anthropic's research system, an Opus 4 lead with Sonnet 4 subagents, beat a single Opus 4 agent by 90.2% on its internal research eval, largely by spending more tokens: about fifteen times the tokens of chat (one agent uses about four), and on BrowseComp token usage alone explained 80% of the variance in performance.

Anthropic says coding is a poor fit: most coding tasks have fewer truly parallel pieces than research. Its [later guidance](https://www.claude.com/blog/building-multi-agent-systems-when-and-how-to-use-them) puts the overhead at three to ten times the tokens of a single agent for equivalent tasks. It also reports teams that spent months on multi-agent architectures, then found better prompting on one agent matched them.

## Parallel agents writing one change make conflicting decisions

Cognition's [Don't Build Multi-Agents](https://cognition.ai/blog/dont-build-multi-agents) is the argument on the other side, and on writes we agree with it. Its second principle: actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results.

Their example is a Flappy Bird clone split between two subagents. One builds the background and the other builds the bird, each on assumptions nobody prescribed. The pieces do not fit. Sharing full agent traces is necessary but not enough, because the subagents still cannot see each other's choices. Their fix is a single-threaded agent whose context stays continuous.

What to split:

- **Safe:** read-heavy work, like reviewing packages or researching sources in parallel.
- **Safe:** whole independent attempts at the same task, with one selected by a check. This is best-of-n.
- **Unsafe:** separate pieces of one write, each made by an agent that cannot see the others' choices.

## Cap spend in the platform, and stop the rest from your code

Our recorded run used two caps:

- **Hard cap:** the fleet's $2 budget. The platform refuses each charge past it, and that agent ends budget-exhausted. Turns already running are not aborted; each is refused at its own next charge.[^cap]
- **Soft cap:** the script polls spend every five seconds and cancels the fleet at $0.20.
- **Result:** six Claude Code attempts on DeepSeek V4.1 Flash stopped at $0.21, so polling overshot by about a cent. All six ended cancelled.

## Each attempt also rents a sandbox

The [sandbox rate card](https://sandbox.tangle.tools/v1/pricing/sandboxes) charges per core-hour and per GB-hour, with a minimum hourly charge. Multiply n by each sandbox's cores, memory and lifetime at those rates, then apply the minimum.

In our round 1, model tokens drove the cost. The twelve sandboxes' compute, plus one refused model call, billed $0.0069. The model tokens on those boxes billed about $0.37. That is one run of short attempts; for longer ones, redo the sum from the rate card.

## Measure the selected answer, not the best one in the pile

Anthropic's [evals guide](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) gives the two numbers to keep apart. pass@k is the chance that at least one of k attempts is correct. pass^k is the chance that all k are.

<Figure n={5} figure={passK} kicker="Worked arithmetic · Anthropic's pass@k and pass^k" headline="At 75% per trial, three trials read as 98% or 42% depending on the metric" />

pass@k is the ceiling best-of-n can reach, and you only reach it if your selector is perfect. pass^k is what a user sees when every run ships. We report accuracy of the attempt our selector picked, and tokens, dollars and wall time per correct answer.

Anthropic's guide also says each trial should start from a clean environment. Leftover files, cached data or exhausted resources can cause correlated failures, and shared state can inflate scores. A fresh sandbox per attempt is the cheap way to get that.

## Spend the 5x only where one attempt fails and a check picks the winner

- **Before any n:** scope the prompt to the whole job, and grade with a check the agent cannot see.
- **You have tests or a checker:** run one cheap attempt first. Add whole, independent attempts in fresh sandboxes only on tasks where one attempt often fails. Put the hard cap on the fleet, stop once one passes, and raise n until selected accuracy flattens on your eval.
- **You have no checker:** run one attempt, on the strongest model you can afford. A vote or judge is a bet of a few points that has to win on your eval. Spend the rest of the budget building the check.
- **The work is wide reading:** a supervisor with parallel readers can pay. Budget three to ten times the tokens.
- **The work is one change:** keep one writer.

Your next step is one eval on tasks your single attempt fails: n of one against n of three or five, at the same dollars, scored by a held-out check on the answer your selector picks.
