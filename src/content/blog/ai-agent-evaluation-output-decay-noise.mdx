We shipped a trace check that flagged "output-length decay" whenever three serial model calls each returned less than the one before.
Three numbers in random order fall that way about one time in six.
Our check was reporting noise as a finding.
We have since found the same flaw in other numbers teams ship on: a tie at 100% on three tasks, a pass on the one test you filtered for, and a judge score nobody checked.
Before any of them picks a release, work out how often it would show up by chance.
Then count independent tasks rather than reruns, require a paired win rather than a tie, run the whole suite, and check the judge against a person.

## Our decay check would fire on one random three-call sequence in six

The idea behind the check was reasonable.
In a long agent loop, if inputs keep growing while outputs keep shrinking, the model may be running out of room to do the work.
The rule looked for that shape across three calls.

The input half of the rule carried almost no information.
A serial agent loop resends the conversation and tool results on every call, so input grows by construction.
That left output direction alone, over three values.
Three distinct values have six orderings, and one of them is strictly decreasing.[^order]

One run made the cost concrete.
Its output length went from 784 to 646, an 18% drop.
The check reported decay, and it was noise.

We fixed it in [agent-eval](https://github.com/tangle-network/agent-eval/commit/b44898e419af67315c5083e68710ec72e9ee0089).
The check now needs at least five serial calls.
No call may return more than the one before it, and the last must be at most 60% of the first.[^ties]
At five calls, direction alone happens by chance in under 1% of random orderings.
The 60% condition then rejects shallow drift that happens to point downhill.

<Figure n={1} figure={chance} kicker="agent-eval · output-length decay rule" headline="Direction alone fires 1 time in 6 at three calls and under 1% at five" />

We left one gap open, and you should know about it.
The analyst's written finding still describes the old rule: "at least 3" calls, with no 60% condition.
Until [that issue](https://github.com/tangle-network/agent-eval/issues/1291) is fixed, the text you read in a finding is looser than the rule that produced it.

The lesson we took is narrow and it generalizes.
For any automated signal, write down what it would look like on random data, and compute how often it fires there.
If you cannot, the signal is not ready to gate anything.

## A tie at the ceiling cannot pick a release

A "candidate scores at least as high as shipped" rule cannot choose between two arms that tie or sit within a task or two of each other.
What to do instead:

- **Know what the rule does on a tie.** It ships the candidate by default. Our recorded gate ran two tasks with one trial each, printed a pass rate of 1 for both prompts, and exited 0.
- **Treat the gate as a floor check.** It can block an obvious regression. It ranks nothing.
- **Require a paired win.** Compare the arms task by task, and ship only when the interval on that difference excludes zero. A zero-width interval cannot establish improvement.
- **No such win means no winner yet.** Add independent tasks and more trials per task before you declare one, using tasks the shipped version sometimes fails.

Here is the gate most teams write first.
It scores the shipped prompt and a candidate on the same tasks, and fails CI when the candidate scores lower.

<Example path="evals/regression-gate" />

The rule did what it says.
It said nothing about which prompt is better.
With two tasks, the mean can only be 0, 0.5 or 1, so any real difference smaller than a whole task is invisible.

We have three recorded comparisons built this way: two prompts, two models and two coding agents.
Every arm passed every task.

<Figure n={2} figure={ties} kicker="Recorded eval examples · one trial per task" headline="All six arms of three recorded comparisons scored 100%, so none of them can rank its pair" />

A tie at 100% is the worst case for a "candidate is not lower" rule.
When every score is 1, any resampled interval has zero width.
agent-eval's [statistical evidence rules](https://github.com/tangle-network/agent-eval/blob/main/docs/statistical-evidence.md) refuse to call that an improvement, and we agree.
Our tasks were too easy to separate the arms.

Evan Miller's ["Adding Error Bars to Evals"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640) recommends inference on question-level paired differences.
When both arms answer the same questions, pairing reduces the standard error of the difference.
agent-eval scores pass/fail comparisons the same way, as a paired difference per task.

If your question is whether a published benchmark score measures what your release needs, that is a different problem; we cover it in [what CodeTraceBench measures and which number should ship your coding agent](/blog/codetracebench-benchmark-measured-wrong-capability/).

## How many tasks and trials do you need, and which pass number?

The short answer, before the reasons:

- **Tasks.** Start with 20 to 50 drawn from real failures, as Anthropic's [guide to agent evals](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) suggests.
- **Smaller effects need more tasks.** Run a power analysis for the difference you care about. A minimum count is not power.
- **Count independent groups.** Tasks from one repository move together, so report groups next to tasks.
- **Trials.** Run several per task, and at least k to estimate pass@k or pass^k. They reduce noise inside a task; they do not add tasks.
- **Report pass^k when users get one try.** At 75% per trial, pass^3 is 0.75³, about 42%, while pass@3 is about 98%.

We think 20 to 50 is the right first target, with the emphasis on drawn from real failures.
Tasks your agent already passes every time are the ones that produced the ties above.

How many tasks beyond that depends on the size of the difference you need to see.
agent-eval refuses a bootstrap interval on a continuous score with fewer than 20 observations, and its docs say plainly that reaching 20 does not establish power.
Miller recommends a power analysis before the comparison.
We have not measured how many tasks a typical coding-agent suite needs; the power analysis for your suite answers that.

Related tasks are not fully independent.
If ten tasks come from one repository, they tend to pass or fail together.
Miller recommends clustered standard errors for that case, and in his example they were over three times the naive ones.

Reruns of the same task help with a different problem.
Answering each task several times and scoring its mean reduces the noise inside that task, which is Miller's third recommendation.
Our statistical evidence rules put the limit bluntly: "Repeating one incident 100 times supplies one incident."

pass@k asks whether at least one of k trials succeeds.
pass^k asks whether all k succeed.
They split fast.

<Figure n={3} figure={evalNoisePassK} kicker="pass@k vs pass^k · worked from Anthropic's 75% example" headline="At 75% per trial, pass@3 reads 98% while pass^3 reads 42%" />

Use pass@k when something downstream can check every attempt and keep a good one, such as a test suite that picks the passing patch.
Use pass^k when a user or a pipeline takes whatever the agent produces on the first try.
A coding agent that edits a user's repository is usually in the second case, so pass^k is the number we would put in front of a release.
Say which one you report.

## A passing target test does not show nothing else broke

Look at the scoring line in the gate above.
It runs only the test whose name matches the task, and scores the exit code.
An agent that fixes that test and breaks three others still scores 1.

We think every coding-agent eval needs a second check that runs the full suite after the agent's change.
Score the task as passed only when the target test passes and nothing that passed before now fails.
A name-filtered check is cheaper, and it cannot see a regression in any other test.
We wrote our examples with the filter to keep them short; a release gate should not.

## Check a judge against a person before it gates a release

Anthropic's advice is deterministic graders where possible, LLM graders where necessary, and human graders for validation.
Start there, because an LLM judge returns a confident number for anything you hand it.
This one scores three replies to a refund request against a policy.

<Example path="evals/grade-replies" />

The recorded run gave the policy-following reply 1.00, a reply that wrongly refused a refund 0.25, and a reply that answered nothing 0.55.
Each composite equals the mean of its two dimensions, so the empty reply scored 1 on honesty and outranked the false refusal.
Whether that ranking is right is a product decision.
We would not have caught it from the scores alone; we caught it by reading the replies.

We would add one step from our own mistake.
The decay check was fully deterministic and would still fire on random noise one time in six.
Deterministic does not mean calibrated.
We have not measured how often an LLM judge mis-scores a whole agent trace, so hold a trace judge to the same check.

Before any automated signal gates a release, do two things.
Have a person grade a sample of the same items, and compare the rankings.
Then work out, or measure on traces you know are healthy, how often the signal fires when nothing is wrong.

## What to require before a number ships a release

Ship a candidate only when it wins on paired, per-task differences, with an interval that excludes zero.
The tasks should come from real failures, sit below the ceiling, and count as independent groups.
Report pass^k if users get one try.
Each task must pass its target test without breaking the rest of the suite.
Any judge in the loop needs a known chance rate and a human-checked sample.
If the comparison is a tie, keep what you shipped, then add harder tasks and more trials before you compare again.

## How we tested

**Decay rule.** The current rule is in agent-eval's [behavioral metrics](https://github.com/tangle-network/agent-eval/blob/main/src/trace-analyst/behavioral-metrics.ts) on main, read on 17 November 2026: a minimum of five calls and a maximum retained fraction of 0.6. The change that replaced the three-call minimum is [the fix commit](https://github.com/tangle-network/agent-eval/commit/b44898e419af67315c5083e68710ec72e9ee0089), dated 31 July 2026. The 784-to-646 run is the one the source comment records as reported decay that was noise; the comment does not give its call count. The analyst's aggregate claim text in agent-eval's behavioral analyst still says "across at least 3 serial model calls" and omits the 60% condition; we filed [agent-eval issue 1291](https://github.com/tangle-network/agent-eval/issues/1291).

**Recorded comparisons.** The regression-gate, compare-models, compare-harnesses and grade-replies examples ran on production on 2 November 2026 with @tangle-network/agent-eval 1.0.12 and @tangle-network/sandbox 1.0.168. The three comparisons used one trial per task (reps 1, seed 42) on 2, 3 and 3 tasks. Their printed output:

```text
# evals/regression-gate (exit 0)
{ shipped: 1, candidate: 1 }

# evals/compare-models (exit 0)
glm-5.3 1
deepseek/deepseek-v4.1-flash 1

# evals/compare-harnesses (exit 0)
claude-code 1
codex 1

# evals/grade-replies (exit 0)
1.00 { answers: 1, honest: 1 } Sorry about that. Send a photo of the damage and
0.25 { answers: 0.3, honest: 0.2 } Refunds are not possible, but here is 10% off yo
0.55 { answers: 0.1, honest: 1 } Thanks for reaching out! We value your feedback 
```

These are small examples on one demo repository. They show how a rule behaves on a tie; they say nothing about which model, prompt or coding agent is better.

**Statistics.** The pairing, clustering, resampling and power recommendations are from Miller, [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640); the "over 3X" clustered-error figure holds for his example. The 20-observation minimum, the zero-width rule and the incident quote are from agent-eval's [statistical evidence doc](https://github.com/tangle-network/agent-eval/blob/main/docs/statistical-evidence.md). The 20 to 50 task guidance, the grader ordering and the 75% example with its roughly 42% pass^3 are from Anthropic's [Demystifying evals for AI agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents); the 98% pass@3 is our arithmetic from the same rate. All sources read 17 November 2026.

[^order]: The one-in-six figure, and under 1% at five calls (1 in 120), assume distinct values in random order. Real output lengths can tie, hit a response cap or depend on earlier calls, so treat these as the chance rate of the shape, not a measured false-positive rate.

[^ties]: The current rule allows two neighbouring calls to have equal output length; the 60% condition still requires a material drop overall.
