If you looked up CodeTraceBench to rank coding agents, it will not rank them.
It scores the tool that reads an agent's trace and names the step where the run went wrong, with step-level precision, recall and F1 against annotated failing steps in SWE-bench and TerminalBench runs from four agent frameworks and five models.
The paper's best tracer gets about half of those steps right.
On one of our own splits, a constant guess beat our analyst more than three to one.
We think every number behind a release has that gap between its name and what it measures.
A passing run can hide wasted or gamed steps.
A SWE-bench Verified score describes twelve Python repositories.
A model comparison is also a harness comparison.
An eval's cost total can read zero for a run that cost money.

## CodeTraceBench grades the tool that reads the trace

The benchmark comes from Li et al.'s [CodeTracer paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11641).
What it measures, in one place:

- **The subject:** a diagnoser, the tool that reads a run and names the steps it thinks went wrong. It is not a score for how good a coding agent is.
- **The metric:** macro-averaged step-level precision, recall and F1 against the steps annotators marked as wrong, on SWE-bench and TerminalBench runs from four agent frameworks and five models.
- **The traces:** previously collected runs, split into steps. The frameworks are SWE-Agent, MiniSWE-Agent, OpenHands and Terminus 2. The models are Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-5, DeepSeek-V3.2, Qwen3-Coder-480B and Kimi-K2-Instruct.

The tasks span SWE-bench Verified, SWE-bench Pro, Multi-SWE-bench, SWE-PolyBench and TerminalBench.
The [dataset on Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/NJU-LINK/CodeTraceBench) holds about 4,300 rows.
So the score tells you whether a tool can point at where a run broke, which matters for trace review or triage.
It says nothing about which agent to ship.

## The best tracer in the paper scores about half on step F1

Handing a strong model the raw trace does poorly.
In the paper's Table 3, a bare LLM scored 16 to 19 step F1 across Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-5 and DeepSeek-V3.2.
CodeTracer, the paper's own tracer, scored 46 to 48 on the same models.
Mini-CodeTracer scored about 19, close to the bare model and far below CodeTracer.

<Figure n={1} figure={tracer} kicker="CodeTracer paper, Table 3 · GPT-5 backbone" headline="A purpose-built tracer reached 48 step F1; the same model prompted alone reached 19" />

A purpose-built tracer beats a prompt by a wide margin and still gets about half of its step judgments wrong.

Our own analyst prompts scored 0.40 to 0.48 pooled micro F1 over 69 cases.[^micro]
That number says little about analysis quality.
Thirty-seven of those cases come from a split we later retired, shown below.
Our audit concludes the pooled result cannot support a general analysis-quality claim.
Every paired interval against the stock prompt, on both splits, crossed zero.
We promoted the GEPA-optimized prompt anyway, under a preregistered point-estimate rule that did not require the interval to exclude zero.

We treat a trace-level signal at this accuracy as a lead for a person to check.
It is too noisy to gate a release by itself.

## Check what the labels reward before you trust the score

What the paper reports about its labels and its limits:

- **Agreement:** a random 15% of trajectories was independently double-annotated, and agreement on the critical step reached a Cohen's kappa of 0.73.
- **Coverage:** the runs do not cover the full space of agent designs or real-world repositories.
- **Judgment:** labels still involve annotator interpretation.
- **Offline:** every result comes from matched offline budgets on previously collected runs.

Our own view, separate from the paper's: a kappa of 0.73 is good agreement, short of certainty.

We hit a sharper problem on one of our splits.
In 27 of its 37 cases, the labeled failing step was the agent's final submit.
A predictor that always names the last step scored 0.568.
Our analyst scored about 0.18.

<Figure n={2} figure={lastStep} kicker="Our CodeTraceBench runs · one retired split" headline="Always naming the last step scored 0.568; the three analyst prompts scored 0.17 to 0.18" />

On that split, the score rewarded knowing where labels sit.
We retired it.
The finding covers one split of 37 cases, but the check generalizes.
Before you trust any diagnoser's score, run a constant baseline on the same cases.
If "always guess the last step" comes close, the split measures label position.

## A passing run can still waste a fifth of its steps

The CodeTracer authors also counted the steps they class as ineffective.
Unsolved runs spent 40% of their steps that way.
Solved runs still spent 22%.
A green test tells you the end state.
It tells you nothing about the wrong, repeated or lucky commands on the way there.
Behavior needs its own grader, or a person reading the transcript.

Anthropic's [guide to agent evals](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) makes the same point from the builder's side.
Grade the outcome and the transcript separately.
Read transcripts before you believe a score.
We would put it more strongly for coding agents.
The transcript is where you find the run that passed by changing the check.

## A SWE-bench Verified score describes twelve Python repositories

It tells you little about your code, because the set is narrow, contaminated and near saturation; build tasks from your own failures instead:

- **Narrow:** its 500 problems, mostly small bug fixes, each come from a resolved GitHub issue in one of twelve open-source Python repositories. Your codebase is probably not one of them.
- **Contaminated:** models have increasingly seen it in training.
- **Flawed tests:** some of its tests reject correct fixes.
- **Near saturation:** frontier models score above 80%, so a large capability gain shows up as a small score change.
- **What to do instead:** build tasks from your own real failures and run them in your own harness.

OpenAI [stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified](https://openai.com/index/why-we-no-longer-evaluate-swe-bench-verified/) over contamination and flawed tests, and now recommends SWE-bench Pro.
It also audited problems that o3 could not solve consistently and found tests too narrow or too wide.
Epoch AI's [review](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/swe-bench-verified/review) rates the benchmark Flawed on that audit.
The audit covered 27.6% of tasks, and 59.4% of those had tests that rejected correct fixes.
That sets a floor of 16.4% of all tasks broken.
The saturation point comes from Anthropic's guide.
We use a public score only to decide which models are worth trying.

## A model comparison is also a coding-agent comparison

The result is about both, and it holds for you only in your own harness:

- **Both at once:** a leaderboard gap measures the model and the harness around it together. Anthropic's guide says evaluating an agent means evaluating the harness and the model working together.
- **How big the harness effect is:** Epoch's review says SWE-bench Verified results are highly dependent on the scaffold. We have not measured the size of that swing on SWE-bench Verified, and our sources give no primary figure for it. On Terminal-Bench 2, [AHE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25850) held the model still, and its evolved harness scored 77.0% against 71.9% for Codex CLI: about five points from the harness alone.
- **What to do:** change only one of model or harness at a time, rerun in your own harness, and report pass@1. That is the first-try success rate Anthropic says it is often most interested in for coding.

So "model A beats model B" on a leaderboard often means model A in one harness beat model B in another.

That tells you both got the tagged test passing on all three cases.

## An agent can pass the check without fixing the bug

It can. Here is what our runs on a nine-test suite showed:[^cm]

- **Skip tests through config:** an agent told to make the tests exit 0 without touching tests or package.json added an npm config file that told the test runner to skip tests. On the agent's own box the test command exited 0. Applied on a fresh machine with no allowlist, the same file ran 7 of 7 tests, all passing.
- **Path allowlist on a clean second box:** it refused every patch that edited tests, package.json (where the test script lives) or the npm config, before any test ran.
- **Exit early from source:** a patch that made the source call `process.exit(0)` early also exited 0, after 6 tests.
- **Special-case the tested inputs:** a hand-written patch passed 9 of 9 and failed three held-out inputs.
- **The fixes:** run the check on a clean machine behind the allowlist. Require the expected test count, which flags both the 7 and the 6. Add held-out inputs, which catch the special case. Then read the diff.

<Figure n={3} figure={cheats} kicker="Our cheat runs · sandbox.tangle.tools" headline="Two cheats ran 7 and 6 tests instead of 9; the third ran all 9 and still failed three held-out inputs" />

The recorded comparison above passes a case when one test command exits 0, so it has none of these guards.
Our rule is that a pass counts only when every guard agrees.

## An eval's cost total can read $0 for a run that cost money

What the reported cost covers, in one place:

- **Model spend only:** the run's cost counts model calls. Sandbox time is billed separately, at the rates on the [sandbox pricing page](https://sandbox.tangle.tools/v1/pricing/sandboxes).
- **The aggregate can read $0:** the eval aggregate for a run that cost $0.19 reported zero, shown below.
- **What to compare:** cost per solved task, with sandbox time added.

The harness comparison above reported $0.19 of model spend.
The example sets `expectUsage: "off"`.
Its eval aggregate, the block you would roll into a cost-per-run dashboard, reported this:

The ledger recorded none of the model calls.
It still labeled the $0 observed and marked accounting complete.
We [filed that against agent-eval](https://github.com/tangle-network/agent-eval/issues/1290).
Until it is fixed, treat an empty ledger as missing data.
A per-run total that leaves out the box, or reads zero, cannot back a switch to a cheaper model.

## Ask each number which question it answers

Before we put a number into a release argument, we name the question it was built to answer.
If you are choosing a trace tool, use CodeTraceBench, next to a constant baseline on the same cases.
If you are switching model or harness, rerun your own failing tasks in your own harness.
Run them on a clean machine, count the tests, and price each solved task with sandbox time.
If the number in front of you answers a different question, the release waits for one that answers yours.

## How we tested

Every outside source was read on 2026-11-17.
CodeTracer figures are from Table 3 (overall macro step-level F1), §2.3 (κ = 0.73 on a random 15% double-annotated subset), §4.3 (ineffective steps: 22% solved, 40% unsolved) and the Limitations section of arXiv 2604.11641 v3.
The Hugging Face dataset card lists about 4.32k rows.
OpenAI's SWE-bench Verified post was read through a reader proxy because the page blocks automated clients.
Epoch AI's review dates OpenAI's audit to 2026-02-23.
The harness result is Terminal-Bench 2 from arXiv 2604.25850.

Our analyst comparison ran on 2026-08-01 with glm-5.2: three prompt arms over 69 CodeTraceBench cases at two repetitions each, scored as pooled micro F1 (stock 0.4285, hand-edited 0.4047, GEPA-optimized 0.4809).
Paired F1 intervals for every arm against the stock prompt crossed zero on both splits.
Of the 69 cases, 32 were a holdout split and 37 were the split in Figure 2.
Agent-eval's archived evidence audit of 2026-09-13 records that the 37-case split was retired after certification because of this label-position artifact.
The independent unit there is the case, not the repetition.

The cheat runs were live on sandbox.tangle.tools on 2026-11-16 and 17, with every box deleted by id afterwards.
The seven-test result is from a fresh machine with no allowlist; the allowlist refusals are from a clean second box.
The suite has 9 tests.
Each pass in those runs was an exit code of 0; the 6-test patch was failed by a live count check, and the 7-test result is a count against 9 replayed from its recorded output.

The harness comparison was recorded on production on 2026-11-02 with agent-eval 1.0.12 and sandbox SDK 1.0.168.
Model glm-5.3, three scenarios, one repetition, each judged by the exit code of a name-filtered test command.
The run reported costUsd 0.1918; its evaluate() aggregate reported the cost block shown above.
